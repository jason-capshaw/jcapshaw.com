An electrical contractor has a bill of materials and four or five suppliers it trusts. The work is familiar. Match each line to a product. Check specs and substitutes. See what's on the shelf at the nearest branch. Pull the negotiated price. Build the order.

Today that's five websites, five logins, a few phone calls, and a spreadsheet.

Now the contractor's purchasing assistant wants to do that work. It already has the bill of materials. It knows the project. What it needs from each distributor is access.

So here's the question every distributor should be asking: can a customer's software do business with us without a person sitting in front of our website?

## Buyers use AI. They still check its work.

[Forrester reports](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/state-of-business-buying-2026/) that 94 percent of business buyers use AI somewhere in the buying process. [Gartner finds](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-20-gartner-survey-finds-sixty-nine-percent-of-b-two-b-buyers-turn-to-sales-reps-to-validate-ai-generated-insights) that 70 percent prefer a fully digital, self-service purchase, and 69 percent want a sales rep to validate what AI tells them. And in [the American Supply Association's latest quarterly survey](https://distributionstrategy.com/2026/10/distributors-see-steady-q4-demand-as-ecommerce-competition-intensifies/), 52 percent of distributors say they're losing business to online competitors, the most cited category.

Three surveys, three populations, three different questions. They don't add up to a funnel. They do point the same way.

The Gartner pair is the one I'd build around. Buyers want to serve themselves, and they want to check the answer. Those aren't in conflict. There's a trust gap between what an agent tells a buyer and what's actually true, and the distributor is the one who can close it.

None of this replaces the fundamentals. The same ASA survey found 81 percent of respondents say competitors compete mostly on price. Price, availability, and fulfillment still win orders. Connectivity just makes them easier to act on.

## Behavior is ahead of infrastructure

The number I find more interesting is the small one. [Octane11 analyzed 25 million](https://www.octane11.com/insights/insights-state-of-b2b-ai-search/) B2B website sessions and found that under 2 percent of search referrals to B2B sites came from AI search engines.

Buyers are using AI constantly. Very little of it shows up as traffic to a supplier's website, even if that share is growing fast.

The data doesn't say why, and I won't guess. But the narrower point holds either way. A lot of AI-assisted buying is happening away from the storefront. A strategy that only improves the storefront is going to miss it.

And it isn't hypothetical. Every procurement platform is building agents right now. I've already seen a large customer pilot one that sends RFQs by email and negotiates off its own price history. Somebody on the distributor's side reads that email and keys it into the ERP. That's the step worth eliminating.

## What the order data already shows

<EcommerceShareChart />

[WizCommerce data on more than 250 wholesalers](https://distributionstrategy.com/2026/10/ecommerce-takes-larger-share-of-distributor-orders-as-companies-scale/) shows ecommerce taking 63.5 percent of digitally captured orders at the largest revenue tier, against 34 percent at the smallest. It's one vendor's customer base, it counts digital orders rather than all orders, and it compares company sizes rather than tracking change over time. It's still a useful signal.

The more telling finding: at three quarters of those wholesalers, the average web order is worth less than half the average order a rep writes. Routine reorders moved online. The bigger, messier orders still run through people, quotes, and a few too many systems.

I've watched reps spend all day moving information from one form into the ERP. That's exactly the work a customer's agent could take on: assembling the bill of materials, checking specs and substitutes, preparing a quote for someone to review. None of it works if the distributor's capabilities are only reachable through its website.

## We've been here before

Distributors have let customers buy through their own systems for decades. EDI, PunchOut, customer-facing APIs. They exist so a buyer can get pricing, submit orders, and exchange documents from inside a procurement platform without ever touching your storefront.

AI is another client on that list. Not a new category of business.

[Model Context Protocol](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification) is a standard way for an AI application to connect to outside tools and data. Basically, an API layer for agents. The [Universal Commerce Protocol](https://ucp.dev/specification/overview/) goes further into commerce: a business publishes a profile of what it supports, the platform advertises what it supports, and the business picks the capabilities they share. It runs over REST, MCP, or agent-to-agent connections.

Neither one knows your contract pricing, your ship-to rules, or your customer's approval chain. That's still yours to model. And publishing an endpoint doesn't mean every agent will find it, trust it, or use it.

What gives you a head start is the service underneath. If your customer-priced availability already runs as a service, exposing it to a new client is a reasonable project. If your pricing only exists on the ERP order entry screen, it isn't.

I've seen the version of this that goes wrong. One inventory API built for the website. Another built for an integration partner. Neither reusable for the next channel. Nobody did anything wrong. The operating model produced the outcome.

<ConnectedCommerceDiagram />

The goal isn't one integration per AI platform. It's one set of commerce capabilities, solved once, reused by whatever client the customer picks: Ariba, Coupa, a homegrown procurement app, a general-purpose assistant, or an agent the customer built itself. The interface changes. The capabilities don't.

## The hard part is governance, not the protocol

The protocol is the easy part. The architecture time goes into what happens once software starts acting on a customer's behalf.

<AgentGovernanceDiagram />

**Identity.** A pricing request has to act as a specific buyer at a specific customer, with that customer's contract and ship-to locations. Anonymous access to negotiated pricing isn't an option. Neither is one shared API key for the whole account.

**Authority.** Reading information, preparing a quote, and submitting an order are different permissions. The customer owns its procurement policy. You need a clear way to know a submission was approved, without rebuilding the customer's approval engine on your side.

**Audit and liability.** When an agent orders the wrong substitute because an attribute was missing, somebody owns that return. Both sides need a record of what was requested, what data came back, what was approved, and what happened.

**Exposure.** Once these endpoints are public, bots will find them. Sending negotiated pricing to a third-party AI platform is a contract question before it's a technical one. Default to scoped, authenticated, customer-initiated access, and watch it closely.

**Exceptions.** The goal isn't 100 percent automation. Get the routine 95 percent running on its own, and send the other 5 percent to a person who knows the product and the account. Customers want to know their request isn't disappearing into a machine somewhere. The people who normally take care of them should still be taking care of them.

None of this is new. It's the same discipline PunchOut and EDI already demanded, applied to a client that moves faster and asks more questions.

## Make it hard for the agent to be wrong

An agent is only as accurate as the data you give it. Stale inventory means it recommends stock that isn't there. A missing attribute means it proposes a substitute that doesn't fit. No price, and the order stalls. I've written about [the cost of bad product data](/writing/the-real-cost-of-bad-product-data) before. A customer's AI raises that cost, because the mistakes happen at machine speed, made by a system that can't lean over the counter and ask.

The biggest problem I've had building with agents is the agent making things up. Most of the fix isn't a smarter model. It's limiting how much the agent has to guess.

LLMs are good with unstructured data. In my experience, they're far more reliable with structured data.

So make the answer checkable:

- Identifiers, attributes, and units as structured fields, so the agent isn't reconstructing facts from a PDF.
- Spec sheets and manufacturer documents as evidence, not a substitute for maintained attributes.
- The source, document version, and last update wherever they matter.
- Verified compatibility marked separately from inferred relationships and unverified substitutes.
- Missing information stated, not silently left out.
- Indicative stock kept distinct from committed inventory. A draft quote kept distinct from an accepted order.
- Real-time price at the moment of the quote or the PO. Cache it everywhere else, and say when it was cached.

And be consistent. I've seen two endpoints return different prices for the same SKU while the website showed a third. No person can sort that out, let alone an agent.

Good data won't eliminate agent errors. It cuts the ambiguity, and it lets the buyer, or the rep, verify an answer in seconds instead of starting over.

## Where to invest first

Stop obsessing over your own agent. Start serving theirs.

A distributor-owned assistant can still be worth building. But if it's the only way in, you're repeating the mistake that put so many distributors behind in ecommerce: assuming the customer will come to you, on your terms. I was in the room for that one. A decade ago, at a publicly traded distributor, the idea that customers would want to buy online was still up for debate.

Customers don't all want the same thing. I've seen one print their online cart and hand it across the counter. You never know how folks want to work with you. Clean product data, reliable pricing and inventory services, and governed access support the website, the sales team, your EDI and PunchOut customers, and whichever AI client wins. If you build your own assistant, run it on the same capabilities.

I think about the build-out as a ladder, ordered by risk:

1. **Read public data.** Products, attributes, documents, known relationships.
2. **Read account data.** Negotiated price, availability by ship-to, order status, invoices.
3. **Prepare.** Draft quotes and orders for a person to review.
4. **Commit.** Submit approved repeat orders, rechecking price, availability, and authority before anything goes out.

<BuildOutLadderDiagram />

Each rung depends on the ones below it. Start with the account management work your customers hate most. One customer I worked with spent hours every month rebuilding a price file with VLOOKUPs and dozens of manual lookups. The same answer was one API call away with an item number and a customer number.

Keep the pilot small. One customer, one recurring workflow, the tool that customer already uses, and an agreed definition of success. Measure how long the task takes, how often a person has to step in, how often prices or availability disagree with the source system, and whether the customer keeps using it. Expand when it's proven valuable and reliable. Not before.

I won't put a cost or a timeline on it. Both depend on the state of your systems and data.

The questions I'd put to a distribution leadership team:

- Can a customer's software understand our products without a person reading our website?
- Can an outside application get customer-specific pricing and inventory, securely, as that customer?
- Can a buyer verify what that application tells them?
- Can a customer transact with us without visiting our site?
- Are we building reusable capabilities, or another disconnected application?

The AI race in distribution won't be won by whoever builds the smartest chatbot. It'll be won by the distributors that are easiest to connect to and safest to trust. Become the most accurate distributor to do business with.

The most connected wins.
